LIFE ON LAND

Forest & Deforestation

Forests are essential for life on Earth. They store carbon, protect biodiversity, regulate climate, and support millions of people. However, global forest loss continues due to human activity—mainly agriculture, logging, fires, and infrastructure expansion. Understanding these causes helps us take action to protect forests and ensure a sustainable future for the planet.

FACT #1

We lose around 10 million hectares of forest every year.

FACT #2

Forest degradation releases more CO₂ than all cars on Earth combined.

FACT #3

More than 80% of deforestation is linked to agriculture

Deforestation

(Forest Removal)

Forests are cleared completely and replaced by another land use — such as agriculture, mining, or urban areas. Tree cover is permanently lost, and the ecosystem disappears.

  • Complete removal of forest
  • Land is converted to a different use
  • Major carbon emissions released
  • Biodiversity loss is immediate and severe
VS

Forest Degradation

(Damage Without Full Removal)

The forest is still there, but its quality declines. It loses biodiversity, soil health, resilience, and carbon-storage capacity, becoming more vulnerable to fires and exploitation.

  • Forest remains, but weakened
  • Reduced biodiversity and soil health
  • Less carbon absorbed
  • Often leads to deforestation later

The greenhouse effect is a natural process that keeps Earth warm enough for life. When sunlight reaches the planet, the surface absorbs part of the energy and releases it as heat. Greenhouse gases—like carbon dioxide, methane, and water vapor—trap some of this heat in the atmosphere, preventing it from escaping into space. According to NASA, this process becomes harmful when human activities add excess greenhouse gases, making the atmosphere trap too much heat and warming the planet. The Geological Survey Ireland explains that without the greenhouse effect, Earth would be about 33°C colder. However, burning fossil fuels, deforestation, and agriculture increase greenhouse gas concentrations, strengthening the effect. The British Geological Survey describes greenhouse gases as acting like an insulating blanket around the planet. More gas in the atmosphere = a thicker “blanket,” trapping more heat and raising global temperatures.
